The mistake I want to talk you out of is the one I made first: optimize
the slow thing, then notice it isn't safe to retry, then bolt safety on
afterward. It works, kind of. It's the wrong order. The order that
actually scales — across teams, across years, across "did anyone
remember to write this down" — is the inverse. Write the idempotency
contract. Then optimize against it.

This post is a short field guide to writing that contract.

## What "idempotent" actually means here

"Idempotent" is the kind of word that lets a team agree on something
while meaning three different things. In production code I find it
useful to be specific about which kind you're committing to.

**Per-operation idempotency.** A single retried call has the same effect
as one call. This is the level interview questions test. It's necessary,
not sufficient — you need it before you can build anything else.

**Per-batch idempotency.** A retried batch produces the same final
state as a single batch, *and* doesn't double-count the rows that
succeeded the first time. This is the one production usually skips. A
batch is "almost done", a worker dies, the next worker re-runs the whole
batch, and the rows from the first run get re-applied. It looks like a
correctness bug; it's actually a contract gap.

**Per-workflow idempotency.** A retried multi-step workflow lands in the
same final state regardless of which step failed. This is the highest
bar and the one that distinguishes "the service is idempotent" from
"the *workflow that uses* the service is idempotent". Most production
retries that hurt are category-3 problems written like they're
category-1 problems.

Before you write code that retries, pick which level you're committing
to and say it out loud.

## The contract — three questions to answer first

The contract has three lines. If you can't answer all three, you don't
have an idempotency contract; you have an idempotency aspiration.

**What is the unit of work?** Not "the request". The *business* unit.
A fill? A position change? A trade-day's worth of confirmations? The
unit decides everything else — the dedup key, the retention window,
the recovery story. I've seen smart teams disagree about it for weeks
after they'd already deployed the "fix"; if the unit isn't on the wall
in one sentence, the perf work is going to land on top of a fuzzy
foundation.

**What is the dedup key?** A hash? A monotonic id? A composite? Whose
namespace? At Axxela the pattern I keep reaching for is a composite
of *(source-system-id, source-record-id, sequence-number)* — three
things, all coming from upstream, all stable across retries. Hashes
are tempting but introduce a "what if two real records collide"
question I'd rather not own; explicit composite keys remove that
question entirely.

**What's the retention window for the dedup key?** "Forever" is wrong
— the dedup table grows without bound and eventually becomes its own
performance problem. "One trade-day" is usually right for fills.
"Forever within a sliding 90-day window" is right when audits need
history. Pick a window, document it, and put a job in to enforce it.
Skipping this question is how you end up with a 50GB dedup table
nobody wants to touch.

## Three patterns I keep reaching for

### 1. Idempotency-key column on the table

For batch ingestion into a single table. Add a unique constraint on
`(source, source_id)` and insert with `INSERT ... ON DUPLICATE KEY
UPDATE` (MySQL) or `INSERT ... ON CONFLICT` (Postgres). Retries
short-circuit at the storage layer; you don't need to read-then-write,
you just write. Cheap, durable, survives crashes.

When it bites: long-tail keys with high cardinality bloat the index.
If your dedup key is a UUID per row and the table is in the tens of
millions, the index is suddenly load-bearing for write throughput.
Profile it before you scale it.

### 2. Outbox + consumer with high-water mark

For multi-system workflows. Write the outbox row in the same
transaction as the state change; a consumer reads forward of a
high-water mark and re-applies idempotently downstream. The
high-water mark is the consumer's idea of "I have processed
everything up to id N", persisted alongside the state it advances.

This is the pattern I reach for at Axxela for broker-confirmation
ingestion. When a broker has a partial outage and replays the same
confirmations on reconnect, the consumer doesn't double-apply
because the high-water mark already moved past them. The cost is one
extra table and a small amount of discipline about advancing the
mark only after the downstream effect is durable. The benefit is
that the recovery story stops being "ssh in and run this script
carefully"; it becomes "the consumer just reads forward, like it
always does".

### 3. Token-bucket dedup on the edge

For request handlers where you can't push the dedup into the
schema. A short-TTL cache (Redis `SETNX`, or a database row with a
unique constraint on `idempotency_key`) keyed by an incoming
`Idempotency-Key` header. The classic Stripe pattern. Useful when
the handler has side-effects in multiple downstream systems and you
need a single place to say "I've seen this request before".

The trap: token-bucket dedup is only as good as the TTL. If the TTL
is shorter than the maximum retry window of any client, you have a
gap. Make the TTL longer than the slowest legitimate retry. At a
trading firm I'd rather over-retain by an hour than under-retain by
a minute.

## The cost of skipping the contract

The first big perf win I shipped at Axxela was a position-recon job
that dropped from ~38 minutes to ~4 minutes. The speed-up came from
bulk writes — `executemany()` against batches of five thousand rows
per transaction, replacing what had been a per-row update loop. The
benchmarks were beautiful. Staging was clean. I shipped on a Friday.

The retry behavior was not clean.

The job had a transient failure mid-run on a Monday morning. The
retry kicked in, started over from the top of the batch, and
re-applied the rows that had succeeded on the first run. Positions
doubled for a window of about twenty minutes. Recovery was hand-
driven and slow because the recovery hadn't been designed — only the
speed-up had. The post-mortem became a single sentence: *we
optimized the speed-up; we didn't design the recovery*.

The fix was a composite idempotency key —
`(broker_id, fill_id, sequence)` — enforced as a unique constraint
at insert time, with `ON DUPLICATE KEY UPDATE` turning each retry
into a no-op instead of a double-apply. With that key in place the
job became trivially re-runnable. The runtime didn't change. The
human-cost of an incident went from "an hour of hand-cleaning state
under time pressure" to "kick the job, walk away".

The visible cost of skipping the contract was twenty minutes of bad
positions. The real cost was the post-mortem, the trust, and the
half-day it took two of us to write the cleanup script that
shouldn't have needed to exist.

## A short checklist before merging perf work

A real checklist, not a checklist-shaped vibe. If any answer is
"we'll figure it out", the perf work isn't ready:

- [ ] What is the unit of work, in one sentence?
- [ ] What is the dedup key, and whose namespace does it come from?
- [ ] What is the retention window for the dedup key, and what job
      enforces it?
- [ ] Where is the contract documented, and who else has read it?
- [ ] What does the recovery script look like — can it be run twice
      safely?

The discipline isn't to answer all five perfectly. The discipline is
to refuse to merge the perf change until the answers exist
somewhere.

## Closing

Idempotency feels like the boring, defensive layer underneath the
real engineering work. It isn't. At a trading firm the idempotency
contract is the engineering work — the *load-bearing* path, the
thing that makes every other optimization safe. Write it first. Make
the recovery a one-button operation. Then make it fast. In that
order, the fast part stops being a source of fear and starts being a
source of leverage.
